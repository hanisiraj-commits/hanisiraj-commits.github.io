Lifecycle grew faster than the store it sits inside.

The one line to take from this

Lifecycle share of revenue barely moved, 32.0% to 33.4%. What changed is the size of the number underneath it. Holding a third of a business that nearly doubled is the result, and it was done while the only product on the shelf was being run down to zero.

03

Rippl sells one machine once, so the welcome flow is the whole conversion engine.

There is no reorder to catch and no subscription to renew. A new subscriber either converts inside the welcome sequence or they sit on the list. 19% more people entered welcome in Q3 and it produced one fewer order.

Read this one honestly

The list grew 78% and welcome conversions did not follow. Part of that is inventory, Pure was thin from mid-July and gone from 20 August, so a third of the quarter's new subscribers arrived with nothing to buy. The rest is the flow itself: it is the inherited build, it sells a discontinued product, and its offer was never designed for the audience Alia is now bringing in.

What it argues for

  • The Nano welcome rebuild is the highest-value job in the account, not a launch chore. A 10% swing in revenue per recipient on 4,000 entrants is worth more than most campaigns.
  • The welcome offer mechanic has to be settled first. A subscription-only discount gives a new subscriber nothing to act on, and this table is what that costs.
  • 2,029 new subscribers are sitting on the list unconverted. They were acquired against Pure and they have never been sold Nano.

Abandoned checkout earns $16.01 a recipient and only 332 people saw it in three months, because a $949 basket produces very few carts. Nano at $599 produces materially more of them. That flow is a volume problem, not a copy problem, and the launch fixes it for free.

04

Campaigns went from a corporate newsletter to a revenue channel.

Campaign revenue rose from $3,217 to $22,119, a 588% increase. In Q2, nine of the eleven sends earned nothing at all.

Customer lifecycle · campaign contribution

Not more sending. Different sending.

Recipients rose 351% and revenue rose 588%, so the extra volume was better aimed, not just bigger. Unsubscribe rate improved while it happened.

Q2 · 11 sends, $3,217

Nine of the eleven were corporate can updates and cut-off reminders that earned nothing. The only two that converted were the 400ml and 600ml bottle release announcements, at $1,898 and $1,319. There was no consumer promotional calendar.

Q3 · 26 sends, $22,119

Thirteen of the twenty-six converted. A real offer calendar ran from June through July, EOFY into pre-order into closeout, and the B2B line kept running underneath it. The last two sends of August were deliberately revenue-free list building for Nano.

Open rate fell from 60.5% to 42.3% across the two quarters. That is the cost of sending to a bigger, colder list: 2,195 profiles have now received 5 or more emails without opening one.

The finding to carry into every launch after this one

  • The same offer, on the same day, was worth 38 times more per person to a list of 280 than to a list of 3,299. Nothing changed but the size of the audience and the fact that it felt exclusive.
  • One send of 280 people produced 36% of the sale's revenue. The other six sends, 11,104 people between them, produced the rest.
  • Financially, the sale is the difference between a good quarter and an average one. Strip $16,061 of campaign revenue out and lifecycle grows 44% instead of 95%.

The teaser is the proof the approach works

The Nano first look went to 4,532 people on 24 August and returned $0, by design. It produced 1,226 engaged profiles in three days. Those people are warm, aware and have never been asked to buy, which makes them the third wave of the launch-day send and the reason the announcement does not have to go out cold.

What this sets up for Q4

  • 1,611 people are warm for Nano before it exists, 385 on the waitlist and 1,226 engaged with the teaser.
  • 2,029 subscribers acquired this quarter have never been sold Nano. They joined against Pure, at a $949 price point, and the cheaper product is the reason to come back to them.
  • The list has to keep growing through September and October. The three weeks from 10 November carried 56% of last Q4, and a list built during that window is built too late.

Worth saying out loud

  • 87% of collected sign-ups reach Klaviyo. On another account audited the same way, only 39% did. A healthy integration is not the default and this one should be checked again after any pop-up rebuild.
  • Alia is now doing nearly all of the acquiring. In July it accounted for 97% of every new subscriber the account added. If the pop-up stops, list growth stops.
  • 768 poll answers have been collected and never used. Alia asks a question before it asks for the email, and none of those answers currently drive segmentation, copy or targeting.

Conversion rate per pop-up, and the winner of the delay test

Alia's API returns account-level totals cleanly, but every filtered query for a single pop-up returns zero, including on pop-ups with thousands of recorded visitors. The per-pop-up split has to come out of the dashboard.

  • On record from the weekly calls: opt-in up 70% against the Klaviyo pop-up it replaced; 7% sign-up against Klaviyo's 5.4% on the 21 July trial, with revenue per subscriber up 75%; roughly 11% at peak in early July; 6.7% on B2C in August against a 10% goal, and 21% on B2B.
  • What we need: August sign-up rate for each live pop-up, so the 6.7% and 21% are sourced rather than recalled.
