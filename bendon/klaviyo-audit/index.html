01 · THE SHORT VERSION

Four things stand between this account and a growth channel.

Two of them decide whether the programme can win a first online order. Two decide whether it can ever win a second.

The website wins one new subscriber in five, and the pop-up is pointed at the wrong objective

Stores do four-fifths of the list building. The one on-site form asks visitors to join a rewards programme instead of buying something, and converts at 3.1–3.8%.

$19.9m of store purchases arrive every year and nothing turns them into a repeat

03 · The execution

02 · WINNING THE FIRST ORDER

The front door is collecting members. It should be making buyers.

Recruiting people into a rewards programme → earning a first purchase

Both versions ran at scale on the same traffic, so the comparison is clean.

Why this happens. A points balance is worth nothing to somebody who has never owned the product. Asking for enrolment first puts an abstract, long-horizon reward in front of a visitor who is still deciding whether to trust the brand at all — so most decline, and the ones who do join are then taught to think about tiers rather than about fit, size and product. That is why the same traffic converts five times harder when the sequence talks about the bra instead of the scheme.

What it costs beyond the revenue. Everyone acquired through a rewards-first door enters the database with no first purchase, no size on file and no product history. That is precisely the data every flow in the rest of this document depends on, so the weakness at the front door quietly caps the ceiling on everything downstream.

What is happening now

  • The only live form is a rewards sign-up307,805 views produced 11,547 sign-ups — 3.75%, against a 5–8% benchmark for a well-built first-order offer.
  • The brand welcome flow sits in draftIts content has not been edited since April 2023. It was switched off around February 2026 and replaced with the rewards sequence.
  • The pre-purchase journey sells the scheme, not the productTiers, balances and enrolment, at the exact moment the visitor is deciding whether to buy anything at all.
  • No first-order incentive exists anywhere on siteSo there is nothing to convert the visitor who is interested but not yet committed.

What it should be

  • The pop-up becomes a first-order offerOne field, one reason to buy today — a welcome discount or a fit finder. No programme, no tiers, no terms.
  • A brand welcome, rebuilt80 years of wired-bra engineering, how to find the right size, the core range, and a reason to act now. This is where the fit story earns its keep.
  • Rewards enrolment moves to after the first purchaseWhere it is a reward rather than a request — and where the customer can be auto-enrolled instead of asked.
  • Every pre-purchase flow re-pointed at one goalA first order. Nothing before that sale should be selling the programme.

The newest rewards welcome, live since 13 July, already returns $3.61 per recipient across its first 4,046 people. The craft is there. It is pointed at the wrong objective.

03 · LIST GROWTH

The file is being emptied faster than the website can fill it.

A pop-up recruiting programme members → a pop-up buying first orders

The growth is real, but it is not the website’s. Stores hand the email programme 78% of every new subscriber. The site produces the other 22% — about 40,000 a year against 132,099 people walking out of the file over the same period. So the online list is not growing into the churn, it is falling behind it, and the gap is being covered by the store network. Australia makes that plain: on-site sign-ups fell 29.6% across the last six months, from 51,657 to 36,390.

Take the stores out of the number and the online database is going backwards.

All of it turns on one pop-up. It is below average.

04 · WINNING THE SECOND ORDER

Every store purchase is being treated as an ending. It should be a beginning.

Chasing store customers with outlet discounts → letting the purchase start the relationship

Two order feeds arrive in Klaviyo. Only one of them is wired to anything.

And it is not a bare transaction.

Every store event carries the data every competitor in this category is trying to buy:

Size · 12JBrand · FayreformRange · JuniperItem · Juniper Underwire BraStore · 1118 RiccartonValue · $79.95

Why this is the most valuable finding in the audit. Everything else here is a repair to something that already exists. This is a channel the business does not currently have at all. Fit is the unsolved problem in this category and the single biggest driver of returns — and the store network solves it, in person, 222,717 times a year. That answer is captured, written into the marketing platform, and then never used again. A competitor selling online only cannot obtain this data at any price.

Why the current approach works against the brand. The only route from store to site today is an outlet campaign. It converts occasionally, but it teaches the store customer that the website is where clearance lives, trains them to wait for a discount, and confines them to the narrowest, lowest-margin part of the range. The rest of the catalogue never gets in front of them.

What is happening now

  • No flow is triggered by a store purchaseWe checked the trigger on all 40 flows. Post Purchase Follow Up and Defecting both fire on the Shopify metric only.
  • Size, brand, range and store are captured and discardedNot one segment or live flow in either account references a size, a brand, a range or a store.
  • The store customer receives the general newsletterIdentical to somebody who has never bought anything — the same discount, six times a week.
  • Outlet campaigns are the only bridge onlineWhich converts on price rather than on relationship, and only ever on clearance stock.

What it should be

  • Post-purchase fires on every orderOne change takes the flow from covering 15% of purchases to 100%. This is the fastest meaningful win in the account.
  • A store-to-online journeyBought a Juniper 12J at Riccarton: hear about that bra — the fit, the fabric, the engineering — then what else comes in that size, then the range the store does not carry.
  • Size-filtered back-in-stock and new-range alertsEvery new-arrival email filtered to what that customer can actually buy, which is the difference between a newsletter and a reason to click.
  • Replenishment on the real cycleThis category reorders every 16–20 months. That makes it a replenishment programme triggered off purchase date and size, not a frequency programme.
  • Brand routingA Pleasure State buyer and a Fayreform buyer should not receive the same email. The vendor field already says which is which.

05 · THE FLOWS

The automation stops exactly where the money starts.

Three stock templates before the sale → a built journey either side of it

Forty flows exist. Fifteen are live. They cluster entirely before the first order.

What is actually wrong with them. These are the highest-intent emails the brand sends and the least considered. There is no split between a first-time and a returning buyer, though the two need completely different reassurance. There is no dynamic product block showing the item that was actually viewed. No size or brand logic, no review-backed social proof, no returns or fit reassurance for somebody buying an engineered product sight-unseen, no escalation across the sequence, and no plain-text founder note — consistently the highest-performing final touch in this category.

Why the gap after purchase matters most. With 149,073 one-time customers on file and a category that reorders every 16–20 months, the absent post-purchase estate is the difference between an email programme and a retention programme. The first weeks after a purchase are when buyer’s remorse is answered, when an 80-year fit story becomes credible, and when a second purchase is either set up or lost.

What is happening now

  • Browse abandonment is a single emailAt $0.34 per recipient against $1.50 for checkout. No second touch, no product logic.
  • Cart and checkout stop after twoThe highest-intent moments in the account, given two touches and no escalation.
  • Post-purchase returns $0.11 per recipientTwo emails, unedited since August 2022, and firing on online orders only.
  • Win-back is one two-email flowAgainst a lapsed base of over a million profiles.
  • Nothing suppresses the never-engaged64,432 people who have never opened remain on file, dragging deliverability for everyone who does.

What it should be

  • Abandonment rebuilt to three or four emailsSplit first-time versus returning, dynamic product blocks, fit and returns reassurance, escalating incentive rather than a discount in email one, plain-text founder note last.
  • A real post-purchase sequenceConfirmation, then the story of the product bought, then care and fit, then complementary product, then the rewards invitation once it has been earned.
  • Replenishment and reorderTriggered off purchase date and size, timed to the 16–20 month cycle rather than a calendar.
  • Cross-sell on what is already ownedA bra bought without the matching brief is a cross-sell the account never makes.
  • Sunset switched onSo the list stops carrying people who will never open, which protects inbox placement for the people who will.

Flows are 37.6% of email revenue on 3% of the send volume. They are already the efficient half of the programme, built badly.

06 · THE CAMPAIGNS

The calendar is spending the list to make the month.

24 discount sends a month → three or four peaks the year is built toward

Twenty-four sends a month into a category that buys once every 16–20 months. Three-quarters of them are discounts.

64

unsubscribes for every 100 people acquired. 89,247 against 138,408 in twelve months. The worst single sends cost 1.2% of their audience — six times the level at which a send should be stopped.

Why the discounting is self-reinforcing. Removing outlet from the site was a deliberate margin decision and the data supports it. But the campaign calendar has since re-created the same dependency on full-price product: constant discounting teaches the customer to wait, which softens full-price demand, which is then met with another discount. The way out is not to stop promoting — it is to compress the promotions into a small number of planned, hyped, finite windows, and to spend the quiet periods building the demand those windows convert.

Why a large part of the database never hears anything. Campaigns go to engagement segments — broadly the 90-day engaged pool of 113,778. Sensible in principle, but applied here it excludes a very large group by accident. Somebody who joins, receives one flow email and does not open it is classified unengaged immediately and receives nothing from that point on. They never get a second chance to become engaged, because eligibility requires engagement they were never given the opportunity to show.

What is happening now

  • 74.8% of sends are offer-ledSale, multibuy, gift-with-purchase and outlet. Education is 4.8% and sent as one-off blasts with nothing behind it.
  • Every campaign goes to the same audienceThe segment does not change with the content, so a product launch reaches the same list as a clearance blast.
  • 64,432 never-engaged profiles receive nothing, everAlongside a lapsed base of more than a million.
  • No systematic testingThree test-named campaigns across the account’s entire history, none in the last twelve months. A year of sending has produced no reusable learning.
  • 0.67% click rate, $0.022 per emailAgainst benchmarks of 1.5–2% and $0.10+. Open rates are strong, so the list is willing — the content is not earning the click.

What it should be

  • Three to four tier-one sales a yearTeased and built toward, so intent accumulates instead of being spent weekly. Bigger peaks, less discounting overall.
  • Cadence down to two or three a weekWhich is what makes it safe to widen the audience rather than keep narrowing it.
  • New product launches as their own revenue peakHype, launch, spike, return to baseline — a second peak type that costs no margin at all.
  • Audience matched to contentA launch to the interested, early access to the top of the file, clearance to the deal-seekers — not everything to everyone.
  • Testing as standing practiceSubject lines on every send; then format, length, complementary product sets, and plain-text founder letters, which also help inbox placement.

What these have in common. Each is a poster: one flat image, one number, one button. Nothing inside is individually clickable, so the email cannot tell you what anybody was interested in — which means it also cannot feed the segmentation and personalisation everything else in this document depends on. There is no product hierarchy, no size or brand relevance, no social proof and no reason to open the next one. The Labour Weekend send is even labelled VIP while going to the entire list.

Why that is the opportunity rather than the problem. Every number above was produced by the offer alone, with the creative contributing close to nothing. That is a floor, not a ceiling — and it is the cheapest thing in this audit to move, because the audience, the offer and the calendar slot all already exist.

The same email, rebuilt.

Afterpay Day Flash Sale, sent 19/03/2026 to 126,747 people. It is the same poster pattern as the four above, so we rebuilt it exactly as we would run it: same offer, same calendar slot, same brand furniture, nothing invented.

Both frames scroll. The full email is inside each one.

Their version — as sent

One image, four buttons. 47.4% opened it, 0.75% clicked. $5,378 in revenue, or 4.2 cents per recipient.

Our version

Same offer, same send window. Every element in it is now individually clickable.

A sign-up bar above the heroSale emails get forwarded and screenshotted, so a share of the traffic they create is not on the list yet. An extra 10% on top of the sale captures that person instead of losing them, and feeds the same front door section 03 is built to fix.
The offer stops being the artworkThe discount moves onto layered stickers over an editorial shot, so the email reads as Bendon running a sale rather than a price poster with a logo on top. Same number, same terms, none of the discount-brand cost.
Four category buttons become four product tilesReal product photography for sets, bras, briefs and mens, each with its own button and its own link. The click now tells you which category that person wants.
Which is the whole pointIndividually tracked clicks are what every segment, every triggered follow-up and every personalised send in this document runs on. A single-image email cannot produce them, so the account never learns anything from the 283 campaigns it sends a year.
A visible reason to clickThe button sits inside the hero rather than below the fold, and the four tiles give four separate entry points instead of one. The 0.75% click rate is the number this is built to move.
Everything else staysThe Afterpay instalment strip, the four service icons, the social row and the full T&Cs are unchanged. This is not a rebrand. It is the same email doing its own selling.

08 · LOYALTY

A sign-up mechanic wearing a loyalty programme’s name.

Points, online only, that nobody climbs → cashback earned everywhere, spent on the site

Eighty-three thousand people join it. Six hundred and ninety-one get anywhere.

Why nobody climbs. The programme only counts online orders, so it cannot see 85% of what its own members spend. A member can buy in store every eighteen months for a decade and stay on Level 1 forever. Add points as the currency — a number with no obvious value — and there is nothing to climb toward and no way to climb it.

The fix: earn everywhere, spend it on the site.

That asymmetry is the whole point. It turns 55 stores into an acquisition channel for the website, without discounting anything.

09 · SCOPE

Everything here is true twice.

This audit was run on the Bendon New Zealand Klaviyo account, line by line — every flow, every campaign, every form and every metric feed, measured against the live API rather than inferred from benchmarks.

The Australian account is a clone of it. Same flow architecture, same brand welcome flow sitting in draft, same rewards-first pop-up, same 283 campaigns a year, same missing post-purchase estate, and the same rewards funnel shape — 25,755 in the database, 2,436 at Level 2, 165 at Level 3. Australia leans on campaigns even harder than New Zealand does, at a 69.6/30.4 split against 62.4/37.6. Every finding and every fix in this document applies to both markets, and ships twice.

What is already right, and worth protecting. The store data feed is genuinely rare — most brands in this category spend years and a great deal of money trying to build it. Open rates run 47.6%, so the list is willing. The segment library is mature and correctly constructed. The cart flow rebuilt in July already beats the version it replaced. The foundations here are considerably better than the output suggests, which is why this is connection work rather than construction work.

You already own the asset. It has simply never been switched on.
