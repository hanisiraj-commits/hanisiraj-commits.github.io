The problem

Retail wins the
sale we paid for

Papi is on a shelf in over a thousand Woolworths and 660 Coles. Those retailers beat us on the three things a shopper actually weighs. They are cheaper. They can post a single jar, while our free shipping only starts at $100 and the average order is $83.87, so most baskets pay freight. And they hand it over today, which no delivery can match.

So the money we spend building the brand gets spent, and the purchase happens somewhere we earn less and learn nothing about the customer.

The list shows it. 63.7% have bought before, so this is a customer list rather than an audience, and still only 36% ever order twice. Lifetime, that is 1.6 orders each, on a jar most people finish inside a fortnight. They are re-buying. Just not from us.

None of which is a small-brand problem. The website has done $2.92m across 45,941 orders since launch, $951k in 2024 and $1.39m in 2025. The list is a real asset. It is just that almost all of it was earned in those two years, and the engine that earned it has stopped.

How we beat it online

Two mechanisms

Give people something retail cannot sell

Launch new product online first

An online-exclusive launch is the one offer a supermarket cannot match at any price. It holds full margin, it converts hard, and it gives the website a reason to exist beyond being a more expensive version of the shelf.

Do it often enough and buying from Papi directly becomes a habit rather than an accident. October and January already proved the mechanic works. The chocolate bar on 18 August is the next one, and it should not be the last.

Stop paying for a sale we then lose

Convert the intent we already bought

Ads, collabs and email do the persuading, then the shopper finishes the job in a supermarket aisle. We close that gap by making the website worth the extra few dollars: merch, an extra discount, gift with purchase, bundles, and offers that quietly pay the price difference back.

Influence.io loyalty is already installed and barely used. Even when someone does buy in-store, we get them into the program so the points land on their account and can only be spent online. The offline purchase then feeds the online one.

The August plan
Build intent, and grow the list.

Two jobs, delivered through three funnels that feed each other. The list gets bigger and warmer, the launch on 18 August converts it, and the flows keep the revenue coming after the spike has passed.

Three funnels

Where August
gets spent

Grow the list
1.75% pop-up rate today. A tuned pop-up sits near 5%.

We have brought in Alia to squeeze the pop-up properly. The BAU test runs the current 10% off sign-up against a sign-up for the new product launch, judged on email submit rate. Whatever wins the homepage stays up until the chocolate bar launches, then we revert to the 10% and keep testing the messaging from there.

Paid gets its own list-building budget: Meta campaigns pointed at a full-page capture experience we build in Alia, running for the whole tease window.

At the same time we tease the launch to the database we already have, hard enough that people join the waitlist. That builds intent in the current list and grows the new one through both funnels at once.

The launch
18 August · online exclusive · the month's biggest D2C unlock

Four flavours, online only, website relaunching the same morning. Everything between now and then is readying the database and nurturing the waitlist we are acquiring, so that launch day converts at the highest rate we can engineer.

  • Teaser emails that withhold the product before they reveal it
  • Existing video cut down into GIFs
  • Countdown timers as the date closes in
  • The free gift revealed as its own moment

The gift is capped at the first 300 orders over $60. Capping it is the point: it manufactures the urgency that converts a waitlist on the day rather than a week later, and it beats the supermarket on value without discounting the bar.

The flows
$59,534 a year · 12% of D2C revenue

The flows are bare bones. They fire, but they do not nurture anyone through a purchase decision, and the data says so: welcome email one returns $6.61 per recipient because it carries an offer, while emails two through five return $0.18 between them. Anything without a discount attached is currently doing nothing.

We rebuild from scratch, starting with welcome, the single biggest D2C contributor. It becomes a four to seven email series that sells the brand first and the first purchase second, rather than firing a code and going quiet.

In parallel we fix the leaks: a proper abandoned checkout flow, then a clone of it retuned as a true abandoned cart. Today one flow covers both jobs and nothing at all triggers on Added to Cart.
