No Fee Day: did free delivery unlock new buyers?

A 48-hour test that carried no product discount at all. We refunded the delivery charge as store credit, which lets us separate friction from price for the first time.

Source: Shopify sales and sessions, Steak King UK · measured at store level against 21 normal July trading days, excluding both promo windows · all figures GBP
01Why we ran it

We suspected delivery cost was blocking first orders

Every other promo we run moves price, so we can never tell whether people bought because it got cheaper or because a barrier came down. This one held product price completely still.

The offer carried no product discount. Customers paid the normal price and we returned the £7.90 delivery charge as store credit afterwards. If sales moved, it moved because we removed a checkout barrier, not because we made the meat cheaper. That is the cleanest read we have had on the UK list.

02The verdict

It worked, and it was the cheapest test we have run

Free delivery removed a real checkout friction for first-time buyers, and it did it on less paid traffic than an ordinary day. The gain landed exactly where the friction was.

03What we set out to test

Three questions, one clean window

Because product price never moved, each of these gets a straight answer rather than a guess.

The questions

  • Is delivery the blocker? Does the £7.90 charge suppress first orders?
  • Can we acquire without discounting? Hold product price and see if volume still moves.
  • Is it repeatable? Build a mechanic we can run again cheaply.

How we judged it

  • Store level, not email. Email attribution catches a fraction of the effect.
  • New customers, not revenue. This was an acquisition test.
  • Traffic-adjusted. We checked paid sessions before crediting the offer.

05The mechanism

Why £79 was the blocked price point

The lift did not spread evenly across the catalogue. It landed almost entirely on one number, and that number tells us exactly what was wrong.

Wagyu Starter Box £99 → WELCOME20 applied → Basket £79 → Under the £99 threshold: £7.90 delivery charged

Our two acquisition mechanics are cancelling each other out. The welcome code pushes the basket below the threshold that makes free delivery attractive, so a new subscriber gets charged for delivery at the exact moment they have just been told they are getting a discount. On No Fee Day, 12 of 25 new customers bought at exactly £79, and that price point's share of new-customer orders went from 29% to 48%.

Two things to keep in view. Pull-forward is not settled: new customers ran 7.33 a day across 26–28/07, and the 24–28 average of 9.4 sits just under baseline, though the promo ran Friday and Saturday while the tail is Sunday to Tuesday, our weakest days. And with no ad-platform access, paid traffic is inferred from UTM-tagged sessions, so cost per acquisition is directional rather than exact.

08What to do next

Fix the threshold instead of promoting around it

The test did its job. The finding is not that we should run No Fee Day again, it is that we should stop charging these customers in the first place.

1. Apply free delivery to the pre-discount subtotal, or drop the threshold to £75. That clears the friction permanently for every welcome-code customer at no recurring cost. No Fee Day rented a fix we can own outright.

2. Re-test clean. Run a matched weekday pair against a like-for-like control to settle the pull-forward question and push the headline rate over the line.

3. Watch the £79 close on 29/07. It lands on the same price point. If it converts, £79 is the box's real acquisition price, which makes this a pricing decision rather than a promo one.

4. Recover the 16 open credits before 24/08. No clicks so far, and there is a £50 minimum spend attached to each one.
